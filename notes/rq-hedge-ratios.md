# Published hedge ratios: can they explain why the sellers' hydro markup stops in 2021?

Written 2026-10-19, from web sources (below); nothing estimated. The question comes from
`rq-price-taking-simulation.md` §16. Net sellers' hydro offers depart from price-taking in
2019 – 2021H1 and stop in 2021H2. One explanation: the stake is overstated in the crisis,
because firms had sold more output forward financially, and the net position n does not see
that. n counts the auction and the physical bilateral contracts (PDBF role 4). If
published hedge ratios rose between the two periods, that explanation gains support.

**In one line.** They did not rise for the year being delivered. Endesa and Iberdrola had
sold forward about 100% of their inframarginal output for the current year in 2020 and 2021,
as in 2022. And most of what they call hedged is their own fixed-price retail book, which n
already counts. So the published ratios give the hedge explanation no support. The part n
misses, financial forwards with third parties, is not published by firm.

## Why they cannot explain the stop

- **The year being delivered was fully covered in both periods.** Endesa: 100% of 2020 (May
  2020), 2021 (Sep 2021) and 2022 (Sep 2022). Iberdrola: 100% of 2020 (Feb 2020), 2021 (Oct
  2021) and 2022 (Nov 2022). A day-ahead offer
  depends on the cover of the day being delivered, so the cover the stake depends on did not
  change. What rose in the crisis was cover two or three years ahead (Endesa's 2024: 46%).
- **The "hedge" is mostly the firm's own retail book.** Endesa says so plainly: its base
  generation is sold "to cover fixed-price contracts with industrial and residential
  customers" (Nov 2021). Iberdrola's 2021 hedge was priced at its retail price (> €70) when
  the forward was €41.50. The net position n already counts this: the retail arm's purchases
  in the auction offset the generation's sales, and an intra-group bilateral contract takes
  both legs out of the auction. A ratio of 100% says the generation is matched by the firm's
  own customers. That is what makes Endesa a net buyer in n.
- **What n misses is not broken out.** Financial forwards with third parties (OMIP futures,
  financial PPAs) are not reported by firm. The regulator verifies, firm by firm, how much
  energy is exempt from the clawback because it is sold forward at a fixed price, but its
  resolutions redact the volumes. For example, CNMC CFT/DE/014/23 (23 May 2024, Endesa
  against REE) gives them as "[CONFIDENCIAL] GWh".

## One thing that is public by firm and crisis-only

**The forward auctions of RDL 17/2021.** The dominant generators (Iberdrola, Endesa, Naturgy,
EDP) had to offer baseload forward contracts for delivery over a year or more, in proportion
to their share, up to 25% of the lowest annual output of their inframarginal plants in the
last ten years. Offered in the first auction: 15,830 GWh, of which Endesa 6,737 and Iberdrola
7,324 (CNMC, via a search summary; *to verify*). These are fixed-price sales to third parties.
They begin in the crisis and may not show in n, depending on how they settle (*to verify*:
physical or financial, and how much was actually sold). If large sales went through, they
lower the true stake from 2022.

**The clawback's exemption pulls the same way.** From 2021-09-16, energy sold forward at a
fixed price (from March 2022, at or below €67/MWh, with intra-group sales counted at the
price passed on to customers) was exempt from the clawback. That rewards fixing prices, but
the current year was already fixed in 2020 and 2021.

## Reading

The published hedge ratios do not support the explanation that sellers stopped because they
were more hedged. They are flat at about 100% for the year delivered, before and during the
crisis, and they mostly measure what n already counts. The explanation is not ruled out for
third-party financial forwards, which no firm publishes. The one piece that can be measured
is the RDL 17/2021 auctions: their sold volumes by firm and how they settle. Without them,
the remaining reading of the stop is that the firms chose it: regulatory and political risk
once prices rose in mid-2021.
